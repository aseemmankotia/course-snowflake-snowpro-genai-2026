## Chapter 12: Full Exam Simulation and Time-Management Strategy — Practice Questions

**Q1.** At the 40-minute mark of the exam you have answered 30 of ~65 questions with about 45 minutes left. Following the time-management strategy taught in this chapter, what should you do?

A) Continue at your current pace, answer with best guess and flag any question you're unsure of, then move on immediately
B) Speed up on every remaining question by skimming answer choices without reading the full scenario
C) Stop and go back to re-read the first 30 questions to double-check them before continuing
D) Slow down significantly to guarantee you get the remaining questions perfectly correct

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

The taught strategy is: never leave a question blank, answer with your best guess, flag uncertain ones for the review pass, and keep moving. This preserves pace without sacrificing accuracy through rushed guessing.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Skimming without reading the scenario risks missing the constraint sentence that eliminates wrong answers, increasing errors
- Re-reading answered questions mid-exam burns time that should be reserved for the final flagged review pass
- Slowing down to guarantee perfection on remaining questions risks running out of time before reaching all 65 questions

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q2.** A scenario question states: 'The company requires that no data ever leaves their virtual network and all inference must occur without any external API calls.' Which technique does this sentence represent?

A) A distractor designed to confuse you and should be ignored
B) Background context with no bearing on the correct answer
C) The constraint sentence that immediately eliminates any option involving external/hosted API calls
D) A hint that the question is testing pricing tier selection only

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Scenario questions typically embed one hard constraint (data residency, cost cap, latency, no-external-calls). Identifying it first lets you immediately discard any option that violates it, often cutting 4 choices to 2.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Constraint sentences are deliberately load-bearing, not noise to ignore
- Constraints directly determine the correct answer, they are not irrelevant background
- This constraint is about data residency/architecture, not pricing, so tying it only to pricing is incorrect

</details>

**Q3.** A question reads: 'Which function call would you use to generate a summary of a customer support ticket stored in a table column, without leaving the platform's SQL environment?' Which category does this question belong to?

A) A generic Gen AI theory question about summarization algorithms
B) A platform-specific Cortex feature question, because it references an in-SQL function call rather than abstract ML concepts
C) A pricing tier configuration question
D) A conceptual question about transformer architecture

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

The question references a concrete in-platform mechanism (a SQL-callable function operating on a table column) rather than an abstract concept, which is the signal of a Cortex/platform-feature question that must be answered from product documentation, not general ML theory.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Generic theory questions describe concepts like embeddings or attention without naming a specific in-platform function or mechanism
- No pricing tier or configuration value is mentioned in the question
- Transformer architecture questions ask about model internals, not about invoking a platform feature

**Q4.** You are building your personal weak-spot review list before scheduling the exam. Which approach best matches the strategy taught in this chapter?

A) Only review the domain you scored lowest on and ignore the rest
B) Rank every domain by your self-assessed practice score, then prioritize deeper review time on your two lowest-scoring domains
C) Re-study every domain equally regardless of your practice exam scores
D) Skip domain self-assessment entirely and schedule the exam as soon as you finish the course

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

The recommended approach is a ranked, evidence-based review: score every domain from practice results, then allocate the bulk of remaining study time to the lowest-scoring domains rather than spreading effort evenly or narrowly.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Only reviewing the single weakest domain ignores other domains that may still be below the passing threshold
- Equal review time wastes effort on domains you already know well
- Skipping self-assessment removes the data needed to target study time efficiently, risking an unprepared attempt

</details>

**Q5.** Your practice exam report shows a scaled score of approximately 700 out of 1000. What does this most likely indicate, given the approximate passing threshold discussed in this chapter?

A) You answered exactly 70% of raw questions correctly and passed
B) Scaled score has no relationship to pass/fail status
C) You automatically passed because 700 exceeds 65% of 1000
D) The scaled score is below the approximate passing threshold of ~750/1000, so you likely did not pass and should review flagged weak domains before rescheduling

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

The exam uses an approximate scaled scoring model where the passing mark is roughly 750/1000 (verify officially) — a scaled score is not a direct raw percentage, and 700 falls short of that threshold, signaling more review is needed.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Scaled scores are not a direct 1:1 mapping to raw percentage correct; assuming 700 = 70% raw is a common misconception
- The scaled score exists specifically to determine pass/fail, so it clearly relates to it
- 700 is below the approximate ~750 threshold, so this assumption of automatic passing is incorrect

**Q6.** With 10 minutes remaining and 6 flagged questions still unresolved, which review approach best reflects the strategy for using remaining time efficiently?

A) Go directly to the flagged questions only, re-apply the constraint-elimination technique to each, and finalize your best answer for all of them before time expires
B) Re-read every question in the exam from the beginning, including ones you already answered confidently
C) Leave the flagged questions as-is since you already selected a guess for each
D) Spend all 10 minutes deliberating on just the single hardest flagged question

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

The efficient final-pass strategy is to go straight to flagged items only, re-apply the elimination technique with fresh eyes, and make sure every flagged question ends with a deliberate final answer, not just the earlier guess.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Re-reading confidently answered questions wastes limited time that should go to flagged items
- Leaving guesses unchanged forfeits the value of the review pass, which exists to improve those specific answers
- Spending all remaining time on one question risks leaving the other five flagged questions unreviewed

</details>

**Q8.** Which best describes the recommended per-question time budget approach for a ~65-question, ~85-minute exam?

A) Spend exactly the same fixed number of seconds on every question regardless of type
B) Ignore time budgeting entirely and answer questions in whatever order and pace feels natural
C) Spend the majority of total time on the first 10 questions to build confidence
D) Budget slightly less time on quick conceptual/definitional questions to bank extra time for longer scenario questions, then use banked time for the flagged review pass

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Since question types vary in reading/analysis load, the taught strategy is to move faster through short conceptual questions to bank time, spend proportionally more on multi-paragraph scenario questions, and reserve the banked time for the final flagged review.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- A rigid identical time-per-question ignores that scenario questions require more reading and analysis than short conceptual ones
- No budgeting at all risks running out of time before reaching later questions
- Front-loading time on early questions leaves insufficient time for later, potentially harder or longer scenario questions

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>
